# LinkedIn outreach

## Purpose

Produce first-touch messages that a busy decision-maker answers, and that the
sender would not be embarrassed to have forwarded to their colleagues.

The failure this skill exists to prevent is not "low reply rate." It is the
message that reads as though it was sent to two hundred people — because that is
what the reader is scanning for, and once they decide it, nothing later in the
message is read.

## Start with the trigger, not the list

This is the highest-leverage part of the whole skill, and the easiest to skip.

Trigger-based outreach converts about **4x better** than cold, with sales cycles
roughly 30% shorter. Two overlapping signals at once ("signal stacking" — a new
CTO plus a stack change, funding plus a hiring surge) push response rates **5-10x**
above generic cold.

So the target list is not the asset. **The triggers are.** A list of 700 companies
with no triggers is worth less than 15 companies where something just changed.

Triggers worth hunting, strongest first:

- **A job posting for the role that describes what you sell.** The budget is
  already approved and the problem is already written down — in their words, which
  you then quote back. Nothing else comes pre-qualified like this.
- **A funding round.** Money exists now, not next year.
- **A new executive in the first 90 days.** New priorities, new budget, wants
  quick wins, has not yet picked incumbent vendors. After 90 days the window
  closes.
- **A live implementation or migration** (ERP, CRM, new plant, new market). Budget
  approved, mandate to change, pain fresh.
- **A public announcement of an initiative** in your area.
- **Hiring surge in one function** — reveals where the strategy is going.

Speed matters: reaching out within about 48 hours of a visible event lifts
response sharply. A trigger you find three months late is barely a trigger.

If you cannot name the trigger, do not write the message. Go find one, or pick a
different prospect. A message with no trigger will read as generic no matter how
well it is phrased, because it *is* generic — nothing about this moment made you
write to this person.

## The one rule everything else derives from

**A message is spam if it could have been sent to anyone else.**

Not "if it was." If it *could have been*. The reader tests this in about two
seconds, on the first line, and the test is fair. Everything below is a way of
failing that test on purpose.

The antidote is not warmth, and it is definitely not flattery. It is
**specificity that visibly cost the sender effort.** If the message proves that
twenty minutes were spent on this company, it cannot be bulk by construction.

## Numbers worth knowing (2026 benchmarks)

Use these to calibrate expectations, not to justify volume.

| Thing | Number |
|---|---|
| Connection request accepted, generic | 12-18% |
| Connection request accepted, personalized note | ~45% |
| Reply rate, typical cold LinkedIn message | 7-15% |
| Reply rate, heavily researched + real trigger | 15-25%+ |
| Effect of ~5 minutes of research per account | 3-5x reply rate |
| InMail under 400 characters | 22% higher response |

Two consequences that people usually miss:

1. **Research beats volume by a wide margin.** 3-5x from five minutes means one
   researched message is worth roughly four blind ones — and it does not burn
   four prospects to get there.
2. **Personalized connection notes are scarce.** LinkedIn caps free accounts at
   about 5 personalized invitations per month, 200 characters each. That scarcity
   is a feature: it forces the note onto the targets that deserve it and leaves
   everyone else on a plain request, which is fine.

## Choosing the shape of the message

There is no single correct opener. Pick by what you actually know.

### 1. Insight / reframe — when you know their business well

From Challenger (Dixon & Adamson, CEB study of 6,000+ reps): sellers who *teach
the buyer something about their own business* outperform relationship-builders
badly in complex sales — 54% of high performers were Challengers, 7% were
relationship-builders. The mechanism is the reframe, not the rapport. Rapport is
the *reward* for a useful reframe, not the road to it.

Use when: you have a genuine, non-obvious observation about their situation.
Avoid when: your "insight" is a statistic they already know. A fake insight is
worse than no insight.

### 2. Poke the bear — when you suspect a problem but cannot assert it

From Josh Braun: ask a neutral question that makes them think differently about
their current setup, with no agenda attached. The point is that you are talking
about *their* situation rather than your product, so there is nothing to say
"not interested" to.

Use when: the pain is plausible but unverified.
Avoid when: **the question is fake.** Asking "how do you handle reporting today?"
when you do not care about the answer is worse than a straight pitch, because it
is a pitch pretending not to be one. Buyers smell it. If you would not read the
answer carefully, do not ask the question.

### 3. Straightforward — when the context already establishes relevance

Say plainly what you do and why it connects to something they are visibly doing.
No question, no hook, no manufactured curiosity.

Use when: there is an obvious trigger (they posted a job, announced a project,
are mid-implementation). The trigger *is* the personalization; adding a socratic
question on top wastes their time and reads as technique.

This is the most under-used of the three and often the most respectful. A person
running an ERP implementation does not need to be led to an insight about ERP
implementations. Tell them what you do and let them judge.

### 4. Artefact-first — when you can build something small

Send the thing before asking for anything. A 30-second demo built from their own
public material outperforms any sentence you could write about it. This has the
highest cost and the highest conversion, so reserve it for the accounts worth
half a day.

## The moves that actually disarm

These are the ones that separate a real message from a good template.

**Name what you are NOT selling them.** "You already have your own ordering
platform, so that is not what I am writing about — it would waste both our time."
One sentence, and the salesman frame collapses. It proves research and it removes
the thing they were bracing to refuse.

**State a limit before they find it.** "Verified only on SAGA; another ERP means
a new connector — the architecture transfers, the timeline does not." Nobody
working an angle volunteers a constraint. This is the single strongest honesty
signal available, and almost nobody uses it.

**Give them an out, explicitly.** Sandler's up-front contract: agree on what
happens before it happens, including "no." "If we cannot help, I will tell you
that too." Offering the exit demonstrates you are not depending on this one
reply, which is exactly what makes the reply likely.

**Ask for information, not for time.** A first message that asks for a meeting
asks the reader to spend the scarcest thing they have on a stranger. A first
message that asks a question they can answer in one line costs them nothing and
starts the conversation anyway.

**Admit when you do not have a hypothesis.** If you genuinely do not know where
their pain is, say so rather than inventing one. Invented pain is detectable and
it is the precise thing that produces the salesman feeling.

**Make the cost of the problem concrete, not the problem itself.** Rackham's
12-year study of 35,000 sales calls found the differentiator in large sales is not
the *problem* question ("do you have this issue?") but the **implication**
question — the one that makes what the problem costs visible. Most people stop one
question early. "Does reporting take long?" is weak. "How long from month-close
until you have per-location numbers in hand?" makes the cost countable, and the
buyer does the arithmetic themselves.

The same study found traditional **closing techniques actively hurt** in large
sales. They are not merely useless — they lower success rates. Anything that
feels like a close in a first message is working against you.

## Selling a capability you have not shipped yet

Normal for a small firm, and legitimate — but there is one bright line.

**Sell the capability. Never fabricate the reference.**

- Say what you have actually built and watched work. Name it precisely.
- Say what you *would* build for them, clearly marked as a proposal.
- Never imply a deployment that does not exist, and never let a sentence be
  read that way by accident.
- When asked "who else uses this?", answer straight. Being first is sellable on
  its own terms: pilot pricing, direct access to whoever builds it, influence
  over what gets built. That is a real offer, and a buyer who wants it will
  self-select.

The reason this is a hard line, not a preference: for a small vendor the entire
case rests on being more trustworthy than a big one. One inflated reference that
gets checked destroys the only advantage available.

Related, from research on how small firms win large clients: the deal is usually
lost not on strategy but on failing to answer *"can you handle this at scale?"* —
and **ambiguity kills trust faster than poor results do.** A stated boundary of
what you do and do not do is a trust signal, not a weakness.

## When the goal is a booked meeting

The default advice — no ask in the first message — is calibrated for cold
messages with no trigger. With a strong trigger you can ask immediately, provided
the ask is shaped correctly:

- **Small and specific.** "20 minutes, I'd show you X and tell you where the
  limits are" — not "a quick chat to get to know each other." An open-ended
  meeting asks them to spend their scarcest resource on an unknown return.
- **Framed as their decision, with the exit named.** "If it doesn't fit, I'll be
  the first to say so." Offering the way out is what makes the yes cheap.
- **Say what they get even if they never buy.** A demo, an honest read on whether
  this applies to them, a number they did not have.
- Never a calendar link in message one. It reads as volume.

## Hard bans

Never appears in a message from this skill:

- Fake familiarity ("Hope you're doing well!", "Just circling back")
- Fake urgency, fake deadlines, fake "I'll close the file" threats
- Generic praise ("love your commitment to excellence") — this is *worse* than no
  personalization because it announces automation
- Personalization that is only a merge field: first name, company name, job title
  in an otherwise identical message
- Sales jargon: circle back, touch base, synergies, leverage, solutions provider,
  reach out to explore, quick chat, pick your brain
- A meeting ask in the first message
- Any claim about your own product that has not been shipped and observed working

## Sequencing

- **Do not message the day they accept.** It reads as automation, because it
  usually is. Wait 2-3 days.
- **Never send to two people at the same company on the same day** with similar
  messages. They compare. When two people at one company are both targets, give
  them different subjects entirely (e.g. data to the CFO, training to HR), or
  stagger and reference the colleague openly: "I spoke with your colleague X
  about Y and it seemed like this touches your area too."
- **Multi-channel lifts replies ~40%**: LinkedIn request day 1, email day 3,
  LinkedIn message day 7, email day 10. Only run this where you have a verified
  email — never on a guessed one.
- **Follow up once, then stop.** Follow-ups perform better written by hand than
  generated.
- **Withdraw invitations with no answer after ~3 weeks** to keep the pending
  ceiling clean.

## Before sending, check

1. Could this message have gone to anyone else? If yes, it is not finished.
2. Is there a question in it I do not actually want answered? Delete it.
3. Is there an ask for time? Move it to message two.
4. Have I said one true limiting thing about what we do?
5. Does the first line survive being read alone, in a notification preview?
6. Is anyone else at this company getting a message this week?
7. Did I claim anything we have not actually built and watched work?
8. If the offer's real advantage is regulatory (data residency, professional
   secrecy, GDPR special-category data), did I say so plainly? For buyers under
   those constraints it is not a feature — it is whether the project survives the
   compliance review at all, and it reframes the sale from "is your model better"
   to "can this be approved."

## Applying this to a named prospect

Work in this order, and write it down per person:

1. **Trigger** — what changed at their company that makes now the moment? See the
   trigger section above. No trigger means the message will be generic no matter
   how it is phrased.
2. **Their job's KPI** — what are *they* measured on, not what the company needs.
3. **What we would actually build** — named, concrete, and something we have
   shipped before if possible.
4. **The honest limit** — what we cannot promise.
5. **Shape** — insight, poke, straightforward, or artefact, chosen from what we
   genuinely know.
6. **Message** — written once, for this person only.

If step 1 is empty, do not write the message. Go find a trigger or pick a
different prospect.
